1. Systems, Properties & Equilibrium

⏱ 45 min 🏆 2–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: None

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: thermodynamics stands on a few carefully chosen words — system, property, process, equilibrium. Once these are clear, everything after this page feels easier.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • System: A quantity of matter or a region in space chosen for study.
  • Surroundings: Everything external to the system.
  • Boundary: The real or imaginary surface separating the system from the surroundings; it may be fixed or moving.
  • Closed system (control mass): A system of fixed mass across whose boundary only energy, never matter, can cross.
  • Open system (control volume): A properly selected region in space through which mass and energy flow continuously.
  • Isolated system: A system that exchanges neither mass nor energy with its surroundings.
  • Property: Any measurable characteristic of a system, such as pressure, temperature or density.
  • Intensive property: A property independent of the mass of the system (pressure, temperature, density).
  • Extensive property: A property whose value depends on the mass of the system and is additive over parts (total volume, total energy).
  • State: The condition of a system described completely by its properties at a given instant.
  • Process: A change of state of a system along a definite path.
  • Cycle: A sequence of processes that returns the system to its initial state.
  • Point function: A quantity whose value depends only on the state and not on the path (P, V, T, u).
  • Path function: A quantity whose value depends on the path followed between two states (heat, work).
  • Quasi-static process: A process carried out infinitely slowly, such that at every instant the system is infinitesimally close to a state of thermodynamic equilibrium — i.e. a succession of equilibrium states.
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium: The state of a system in which no spontaneous change of any property occurs; it exists only when thermal, mechanical and chemical equilibrium hold simultaneously.
  • Two ways to look at matter. Stand back and watch the big picture — that is the macroscopic approach. Zoom in and track every molecule — that is the microscopic approach. To know your tyre pressure you never count air molecules; a gauge reads the average push of billions of them. That average view is classical thermodynamics, and this course uses it throughout. The molecule-tracking method (statistical thermodynamics) is powerful but needs heavy mathematics. (MO2024 P1 Q.1(a), MO2025 MID Q.1(a))

Control volume: instead of following a blob of gas around, draw an imaginary fence around a machine and watch whatever crosses it. That fenced region is the control volume; the fence is the control surface. Every steady-flow device is analysed this way.
  • State and properties. The state is the condition of the system right now, described by its properties (pressure, volume, temperature…). Fix any two independent intensive properties and the whole state is fixed.
  • Intensive or extensive? Cut the system into two halves. If the property keeps the same value in each half → intensive (pressure, temperature, density). If each half now holds only half of it → extensive (mass, total volume, total energy). Anything divided by mass (v, u, h) becomes specific and acts intensive. (MO2022 END Q.1(a): enthalpy H is extensive; specific enthalpy h is intensive.)
  • Process and cycle. A process changes the state along a definite path. A cycle is a chain of processes that returns the system to its starting state.
  • Point functions vs path functions. A point function answers "where are you?" — P, V, T, u, h, s depend only on the state, never on the route. A path function answers "how much happened along the way?" — work and heat. Drive Delhi → Jaipur: the destination coordinates don't care which road you took, but the kilometres on the odometer do. That is why we write dP and dV (exact differentials) but δW and δQ (inexact). (MO2025 MID Q.3(a))
  • Quasi-static process (MO2024 P2 Q.1(a), MO2025 MID Q.2(a)). "Quasi-static" means almost static: the process runs so slowly that the system stays essentially balanced at every moment. Exam-ready features: infinitely slow; every intermediate state is an equilibrium state; the whole path shows on the P–V diagram — which is the only reason we may compute $W=\int P\,dV$; and it is the ideal version of a real process, the seed of reversibility.
  • Simple compressible substance: one whose only important work mode is expanding against pressure (no electrical, magnetic or surface effects). Two independent intensive properties fix its complete state.
  • Units (SI) in ten seconds. Base units: kilogram, metre, second, kelvin. Everything else builds from them: F = ma gives the newton (kg·m/s²); pressure = N/m² (pascal); energy = N·m (joule); power = J/s (watt).
  • Thermodynamic equilibrium (MO2024 P1 Q.2(a)) means the system has fully settled — nothing changes if left alone. All three conditions must hold together: thermal (same T everywhere), mechanical (no unbalanced forces, uniform p) and chemical (no composition drift or reaction). Uniform temperature alone is just thermal equilibrium — that is the difference the paper asks about.

📘 Worked Example

MID MO2022 Q.1(b): A tank has two rooms separated by a membrane. Room A holds 2 kg of air with specific volume 0.5 m³/kg. Room B holds 0.75 m³ of air of density 0.8 kg/m³. The membrane breaks and the air settles into one uniform state. Find the final specific volume.

Room A volume: $V_A = m_A v_A = 2 \times 0.5 = 1\ \text{m}^3$ Room B mass: $m_B = \rho_B V_B = 0.8 \times 0.75 = 0.6\ \text{kg}$ After rupture, treat both rooms as ONE closed system: total mass $= 2.6$ kg, total volume $= 1.75$ m³ $v_f = \dfrac{1.75}{2.6} \approx 0.673\ \text{m}^3/\text{kg}$

2. Zeroth Law, Ideal Gas & Pure Substances

⏱ 60 min 🏆 2–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 1

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: the Zeroth law gives us temperature, the ideal-gas law links p, V and T for gases far from condensation, and the steam tables tell you exactly where water sits between liquid and vapour.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Zeroth law: When two bodies A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This law establishes temperature as a property.
  • Ideal gas: A gas that obeys the equation of state pV = mRT at all states.
  • Pure substance: A substance of homogeneous and invariable chemical composition throughout its mass, in every phase (water qualifies even as liquid + vapour).
  • Saturated liquid: Liquid at the boiling temperature corresponding to the given pressure — about to vaporise.
  • Dry saturated vapour: Steam that has just completed vaporisation and holds no liquid droplets.
  • Quality (dryness fraction), x: The ratio of the mass of vapour to the total mass of a liquid–vapour mixture.
  • Superheated vapour: Vapour at a temperature higher than the saturation temperature for its pressure.
  • Sensible heat: Heat that changes the temperature of a body without altering its phase.
  • Latent heat: Heat absorbed or rejected during a change of phase at constant temperature (e.g. h_fg for boiling).

Zeroth law — where temperature comes from

  • The law: if A is in thermal balance with C, and B is separately in thermal balance with C, then A and B are in thermal balance with each other.
  • Why it matters: this quiet little statement makes "temperature" a real, comparable property. It also explains the thermometer: let C be the thermometer. Touch it to A → get a reading. Touch it to B → same reading. Therefore A and B have equal temperature, even though they never touched each other.

Ideal gas

$pV = mRT$ (mass form) $pV = n\,\bar{R}T$ (mole form) $pv = RT$ (per kg) Universal constant: $\bar R = 8.314\ \text{kJ/(kmol\cdot K)}$ Per-kg: $R = \bar R / M$; for air $M = 28.97 \Rightarrow R = 0.287\ \text{kJ/kg\cdot K}$
  • An ideal gas obeys this at every state. Real gases follow it well when they are far from turning liquid — low pressure, high temperature.
  • Real-gas aside: nearer condensation we correct with van der Waals: $(p + a/v^2)(v - b) = RT$, where $a$ accounts for molecules attracting each other and $b$ for the space they occupy. One paper asked for displacement work with exactly this equation — see Topic 3.

Pure substances, phases & steam tables

  • Pure substance (MO2024 P1 Q.3(a)): matter whose chemical make-up is identical everywhere, in every phase. Water qualifies even as a liquid-plus-vapour mixture — both parts are H₂O. Air strictly fails: on cooling, nitrogen and oxygen condense at different temperatures, so the phases differ in composition. As a plain gas, though, air is treated as pure for most problems.
  • The phase journey of water: compressed liquid → saturated liquid (about to boil) → wet mixture (liquid + vapour together) → saturated vapour (just finished boiling) → superheated vapour. Boiling happens at one fixed temperature for each pressure — the saturation temperature.
  • Quality x measures how much of a wet mixture is vapour, by mass: x = 0 means all liquid, x = 1 means all vapour.
$v = v_f + x\,v_{fg}, \qquad h = h_f + x\,h_{fg}, \qquad s = s_f + x\,s_{fg}$ (f = saturated liquid, g = saturated vapour, fg = difference)
Steam-table algorithm — use it in every numerical. Step 1: compare your given h with the saturated table at that pressure. Below hf → compressed liquid (take h ≈ hf at the same T). Between hf and hg → wet mixture → solve for x. Above hg → superheated → interpolate in the superheat table. Step 2–3 follow from whichever case you land in.

3. Work, Heat & Their Comparison

⏱ 45 min 🏆 2–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 1

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: work and heat are energy crossing the boundary while things happen. They belong to processes, not states — a system never "contains" work or heat.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Work (thermodynamic definition): Work is done by a system during a given operation if the sole effect of the interaction on things external to the system could be reduced to the raising of a weight.
  • Displacement (moving-boundary) work: Boundary work done by a gas expanding against a piston; for a quasi-static process, W = ∫p dV.
  • Free expansion: The unrestrained expansion of a gas into vacuum; no work is done, and for an insulated ideal gas there is no temperature change.
  • Heat: The form of energy that crosses a system boundary solely because of a temperature difference between the system and its surroundings.
  • Adiabatic process: A process during which no heat crosses the system boundary.

Work — the thermodynamic definition

  • Definition (MO2023 Q.1(a)): work is done by a system if its only effect outside could have been the lifting of a weight. Simpler view: work is energy crossing the boundary driven by anything except a temperature difference.
  • Three habits of work: it shows up only at the boundary; it depends on the whole path (δW, not dW); saying "the system contains work" is meaningless — it is transit energy, not stored energy. Positive sign = work done by the system.

Moving-boundary work

$\delta W = p\,dV \quad\Rightarrow\quad W_{1-2} = \int_{1}^{2} p\,dV$ Only valid when the process is quasi-static — otherwise there is no single p to use. Graph meaning: area under the curve on the p–V diagram.

4. First Law, Internal Energy & Non-flow Processes

⏱ 75 min 🏆 2–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topics 1–3

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: energy is never created or destroyed. Heat added minus work delivered equals the change in stored energy of the system.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • First law (cyclic statement): When a closed system executes a complete cycle, the net heat interaction equals the net work interaction: √δQ = √δW.
  • First law (non-cyclic statement): For a closed system changing state, Q − W = ΔU — heat supplied minus work done equals the gain in stored internal energy.
  • Internal energy: The sum of the kinetic and potential energies of the molecules of a system; a point function whose change depends only on the end states.
  • Law of conservation of energy: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, although it may be converted from one form into another.
  • PMM-1 (perpetual motion machine of the first kind): A hypothetical device that delivers work continuously without consuming any energy — impossible by the first law.
  • Joule's experiment: stirring water with a paddle wheel warmed it by exactly the same amount as supplying heat would have. Conclusion: heat and work are two currencies of the same thing — energy — with a fixed exchange rate.
  • First law for a cycle: go around any complete loop and you return to the starting state, so stored energy change is zero: $$\oint \delta Q = \oint \delta W$$ Net heat in = net work out. (MO2022 END Q.2(a), MO2023 Q.4(a))
  • PMM-1 (perpetual motion machine of the first kind): an imaginary machine that keeps producing work without any energy input. The first law forbids it — no machine creates energy from nothing.
  • For a process (not a cycle): $$Q - W = \Delta U$$ valid for a closed system with KE/PE neglected. U is the internal energy.
  • Why internal energy is a property (MO2022 MID Q.4(a), MO2024 P1 Q.4(a)): take states 1 → 2 by path A, and back 2 → 1 by path B, forming a loop. The cyclic law gives $\Delta U_A = \Delta U_B$ — otherwise the loop could manufacture energy from nothing. Since every path between the same end points gives the same ΔU, u depends only on state: it is a property. Physically, u is the sum of molecular kinetic energies plus inter-molecular potential energy.
  • Closed-system bookkeeping: $E_2-E_1 = Q-W$ with $E = U + \text{KE} + \text{PE}$; for stationary systems it shrinks to $Q = \Delta U + W$. Consequences worth quoting in exams: heat and work are mutually convertible; an isolated system's energy never changes; PMM-1 is impossible.

5. Enthalpy & Specific Heats

⏱ 30 min 🏆 2–3 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 4

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: cv heats gas in a locked box; cp heats it under a free piston — so cp is always bigger, because part of the heat escapes as expansion work. Enthalpy bundles internal energy with the "push-along" cost pv.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Enthalpy: The sum of the internal energy and the flow (pressure–volume) work of a fluid: h = u + pv.
  • Specific heat at constant volume: The heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass by one degree at constant volume: c_v = (∂u/∂T)_v.
  • Specific heat at constant pressure: The heat required to raise the temperature of unit mass by one degree at constant pressure: c_p = (∂h/∂T)_p.
  • Mayer’s relation: For an ideal gas, c_p − c_v = R.
  • Enthalpy: $h=u+pv$ (kJ/kg). Why invent it? A flowing stream must push the fluid ahead of it — carrying energy $pv$ per kg called flow work. So what arrives is $u+pv=h$. Bonus: for any constant-pressure process, $Q=\Delta H$.
  • Specific heats: $$c_v=\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial T}\right)_v,\qquad c_p=\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial T}\right)_p$$ heat added per kg per degree rise, at fixed volume / fixed pressure. $c_p>c_v$ always.
  • Mayer's relation (three lines): for an ideal gas $h=u+RT$; differentiate: $dh=du+R\,dT$; divide by dT: $$c_p-c_v=R,\qquad \gamma=\frac{c_p}{c_v},\qquad c_v=\frac{R}{\gamma-1},\quad c_p=\frac{\gamma R}{\gamma-1}$$ Air numbers: $c_v=0.718$, $c_p=1.005$ kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.4.
  • Ideal-gas shortcuts that always work: $du=c_vdT$ and $dh=c_pdT$ hold during ANY ideal-gas process, because u and h depend on temperature alone.
  • Sensible vs latent heat: sensible heat changes temperature ($mc\Delta T$); latent heat changes phase at constant temperature ($mh_{fg}$ for boiling).

6. Control Volume — SFEE & Applications

⏱ 75 min 🏆 3–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topics 4–5

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: the steady-flow energy equation is energy bookkeeping for machines: whatever flows in must flow out — as enthalpy, motion, height, heat or shaft work.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Steady-flow process: A flow process in which conditions at any point of the control volume do not vary with time, and the inlet mass flow rate equals the outlet mass flow rate.
  • Flow work: The work associated with pushing a fluid element across a control surface; per unit mass it equals pv.
  • Steady-flow energy equation (SFEE): For a steady-flow system, Q̇ − Ẇ = ṁ[(h₂−h₁) + (V₂²−V₁²)/2 + g(z₂−z₁)] — energy enters as enthalpy, motion and height, and leaves as enthalpy, motion, height and shaft work.
  • Throttling: Flow through a partly opened valve or constriction with no heat exchange, no work, and negligible KE/PE change; enthalpy stays constant (h₁ = h₂, isenthalpic).
  • Flow work: to enter a machine, each kilogram must push the fluid ahead of it. That pushing costs $pv$ per kg — the "entry fee" carried by every stream. Add it to internal energy and you get enthalpy.
  • Steady-flow assumptions: mass rate in = mass rate out and neither changes with time; conditions at any fixed point never change; heat and work rates are constant; properties are uniform across each inlet/outlet section.
  • The equation (derivation asked in MO2024 P1 Q.5(a)): apply the first law to the control volume per unit time:
$$\dot Q-\dot W=\dot m\Big[(h_2-h_1)+\frac{V_2^2-V_1^2}{2}+g(z_2-z_1)\Big]$$ Left side: heat added minus shaft work delivered. Right side: rise in enthalpy + kinetic energy + potential energy of the stream. Per-kg form: divide everything by ṁ. Unsteady (general) version adds a storage term $dE_{cv}/dt$ on the left.

7. Limits of First Law · Engines & Fridges · Statements

⏱ 60 min 🏆 2–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Module 2

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: the first law counts energy but knows no direction — a cooling cup and a self-warming cup both balance its books. The second law adds direction: heat flows downhill on its own, and no engine is perfect.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Heat engine: A continuously operating device that receives heat from a source, converts part of it into net work, and rejects the remainder to a sink.
  • Kelvin–Planck statement: It is impossible to construct an engine operating on a cycle whose sole effect is the extraction of heat from a single reservoir and the delivery of an equivalent amount of work.
  • Clausius statement: It is impossible to construct a device operating on a cycle whose sole effect is the transfer of heat from a cooler body to a hotter body.
  • Refrigerator: A cyclic device whose object is to extract heat Q_L from a low-temperature space; COP_R = Q_L/W.
  • Heat pump: A cyclic device whose object is to supply heat Q_H to a high-temperature space; COP_HP = Q_H/W = COP_R + 1.
  • PMM-2 (perpetual motion machine of the second kind): A hypothetical engine that exchanges heat with a single reservoir and converts it completely into work — forbidden by the second law.
  • Three limitations of the first law: (1) it says nothing about direction — it cannot tell spontaneous from impossible; (2) no cap on conversion — by itself it allows 100 % heat→work in a cycle, which reality forbids; (3) no feasibility test — it cannot say whether a proposed machine will actually run. The second law answers all three.
  • Reservoir language: a thermal reservoir keeps constant temperature however much heat it exchanges. Source = we take heat FROM it; sink = we dump heat INTO it.
Heat engine: $\eta=\dfrac{W}{Q_H}=1-\dfrac{Q_L}{Q_H}$ Refrigerator: $COP_R=\dfrac{Q_L}{W}$ Heat pump: $COP_{HP}=\dfrac{Q_H}{W}=COP_R+1$ Same hardware, different goal: fridge protects the cold box, HP heats the warm room.
  • Kelvin–Planck statement: no cyclic engine can take heat from ONE reservoir and turn ALL of it into work. Every engine must reject something to a sink.
  • Clausius statement: no cyclic device can move heat from cold to hot as its only effect. A refrigerator is fine — because it also consumes work, that's not the "sole effect".
  • PMM-2: the Kelvin–Planck violator — an engine beating one reservoir. Just as impossible as PMM-1.
  • Why the statements are equivalent (MO2025 END Q.3(a)): prove each violates the other by coupling machines. Suppose Clausius is broken: a magic device lifts $Q_L$ from cold to hot free of cost. Now run a normal heat engine between the same reservoirs taking $Q_H$ and rejecting exactly $Q_L$ into the cold body. Composite effect: net work out, single reservoir, nothing else changed → Kelvin–Planck broken too. Reverse the roles for the other direction. Since each violation drags down the other, the statements stand or fall together — equivalent.

8. Carnot Cycle, Theorems & Corollaries

⏱ 60 min 🏆 5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 7

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: the Carnot cycle is the best any engine can do between two temperatures — and its efficiency depends ONLY on those two temperatures, nothing else.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Carnot cycle: A completely reversible cycle made of two reversible isothermal processes and two reversible adiabatic processes.
  • Carnot theorem: Of all heat engines working between the same two reservoirs, no engine can be more efficient than a reversible engine.
  • Corollary: All reversible engines working between the same two reservoirs have equal efficiency, η = 1 − T_L/T_H.
  1. 1–2 Isothermal expansion at T_H: gas expands while pressed against the hot source, drawing $Q_H$ at constant temperature.
  2. 2–3 Adiabatic expansion: insulated now — expansion cools the gas from $T_H$ down to $T_L$.
  3. 3–4 Isothermal compression at T_L: compressed while touching the cold sink, dumping $Q_L$.
  4. 4–1 Adiabatic compression: insulated again, temperature climbs back to $T_H$, closing the loop.
  • On p–V this traces a lens between two isotherms and two adiabats. On T–s it is a perfect rectangle: width Δs, so enclosed area = $(T_H-T_L)\Delta s$ = net work.
  • Efficiency derivation: the two isotherms move equal entropy amounts, so $\frac{Q_H}{T_H}=\frac{Q_L}{T_L}$:
$$\eta_C=1-\frac{T_L}{T_H}\qquad COP_{R,C}=\frac{T_L}{T_H-T_L},\quad COP_{HP,C}=\frac{T_H}{T_H-T_L}$$
  • Carnot theorem (MO2022 END Q.3(b)): no engine between two given reservoirs beats a reversible one.
  • Proof in four moves: assume engine E beats reversible R → drive R backwards using E → size them so R-back cancels E's rejection at the sink → the pair then exchanges heat with only ONE reservoir yet delivers net work → that is PMM-2, forbidden → assumption false. ∎
  • Corollary: run two reversible engines against each other; neither may win, so all reversible engines between the same reservoirs have identical efficiency.
  • Use: Carnot efficiency is the yardstick — real engines are graded against it (exactly how Topic 7's classification numerical works).

📘 Worked Example

END MO2025 Q.3(b): A reversible power cycle takes $Q_1$ at $T_1$, rejects $Q_2$ at $T_2$; it drives a reversible heat pump that lifts $Q_2$ from $T_2$ and delivers $Q_3$ at $T_3$. Find $Q_3/Q_1$.

9. Reversibility & the Absolute Temperature Scale

⏱ 30 min 🏆 2–5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 8

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: a reversible process is one whose movie can be played backwards without anyone noticing — system AND world return untouched. Real life always leaves footprints; thermodynamics calls them irreversibilities.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Reversible process: A process which, having occurred, can be reversed so that the system AND the surroundings return to their initial states, leaving no trace anywhere.
  • Irreversible process: A process that cannot be reversed without leaving permanent changes in the surroundings.
  • Internal irreversibility: Irreversibility arising within the system itself — fluid friction, eddies, unrestrained expansion.
  • External irreversibility: Irreversibility occurring at the system boundary or beyond — chiefly heat transfer through a finite temperature difference.
  • Absolute thermodynamic temperature scale: A temperature scale, independent of any working substance, defined by the heat-ratio equality Q_H/Q_L = T_H/T_L of reversible engines.
  • Conditions for reversibility: infinitely slow motion (quasi-static), no friction anywhere, heat exchanged only across vanishingly small temperature differences, no mixing or free expansion.
  • Usual suspects of irreversibility: friction, unresisted expansion, finite-ΔT heat transfer, fluid mixing, combustion and electrical resistance.
  • Internal irreversibility happens inside the working fluid itself — eddies, fluid friction, rapid non-uniform heating.
  • External irreversibility happens at the boundary with the surroundings — chiefly heat crossing a big temperature gap, or mechanical friction at bearings and seals.
  • Real engines carry both kinds; idealising both away gives the reversible benchmark used throughout Module 3.

Absolute thermodynamic temperature scale

  • Kelvin's trick: use reversible-engine heat ratios to DEFINE temperature — no thermometer substance needed. Any reversible engine obeys
$$\frac{Q_H}{Q_L}=\frac{\Phi(\theta_H)}{\Phi(\theta_L)}\equiv\frac{T_H}{T_L}$$ Pick the simple choice $\Phi(t)=at+b$: that IS the kelvin scale. Anchor: triple point of water = 273.16 K; then any unknown T follows from measured heat ratios alone. The scale is universal — independent of mercury, alcohol, hydrogen or anything else.

$20/14.6 = 1.370 = \dfrac{100a+b}{b}$ $\Rightarrow 100a = 0.370\,b \Rightarrow \dfrac ba = 270.3$ Absolute zero is where $\Phi=0$: $at+b=0 \Rightarrow t=-\dfrac ba=\mathbf{-270.3^{\circ}\text{C}}$ (the small gap vs −273.15 comes from assuming linearity over this range)

10. Entropy, Clausius Inequality & the Entropy Principle

⏱ 90 min 🏆 5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Module 3

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: entropy keeps score of "spread-out" energy. The universe's entropy never decreases — that one line decides which processes are possible.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Entropy: A property of a system defined by dS = δQ_rev/T; it quantifies the energy that cannot be converted into work.
  • Clausius inequality: For any cycle, √δQ/T ≤ 0, equality holding only when every step of the cycle is reversible.
  • Principle of increase of entropy: The entropy of the universe (system + surroundings, an isolated combination) always increases in real processes and remains constant only for reversible ones: ΔS_universe ≥ 0.
  • Entropy generation: Entropy created by irreversibilities within a system; zero for reversible processes, strictly positive for real ones.
  • Isentropic process: A process in which the entropy remains constant — the reversible adiabatic process.
  • Clausius inequality — asked word-for-word in both MO2024 P2 and MO2025 END: $$\oint\frac{\delta Q}{T}\le0 \qquad (=0\ \text{reversible},\ \lt0\ \text{irreversible})$$
Proof outline you can write in the exam: run any engine between a reservoir at $T_0$ (the hottest thing around) and a boundary region at temperature T. Attach a small reversible Carnot refrigerator between them to shuffle the heat δQ at T. The combined plant now talks to a single reservoir $T_0$, so by Kelvin–Planck its net work cannot be positive. Turning that crank algebraically yields $\oint\delta Q/T\le0$, equality only when every step is reversible.
  • Entropy is born here: for reversible cycles $\oint\delta Q_{rev}/T=0$ exactly — an exact differential. Define $$dS=\frac{\delta Q_{rev}}{T}$$ Same two-path argument as for u: any loop gives ∮dS = 0, so S depends only on state → property. (MO2022 END Q.4(a))
  • Working rule from the inequality: for any process $\Delta S\ge\int\delta Q/T$; equal only if reversible.
  • Ready-made ΔS formulas:
Heat at constant T: $\Delta S=Q/T$ Constant p heating: $\Delta S=mc_p\ln(T_2/T_1)$ Constant v heating: $\Delta S=mc_v\ln(T_2/T_1)$ Ideal gas (both forms): $$\Delta S=m\Big[c_v\ln\tfrac{T_2}{T_1}+R\ln\tfrac{V_2}{V_1}\Big]=m\Big[c_p\ln\tfrac{T_2}{T_1}-R\ln\tfrac{p_2}{p_1}\Big]$$ Reversible adiabatic: $\Delta S=0$ (that's what "isentropic" means) Phase change: $\Delta S=h_{fg}/T_{sat}$ per kg
  • Tds equations (first law written reversibly): $Tds=du+p\,dv$ and $Tds=dh-v\,dp$. These bridge into Module 5.
  • Entropy balance (accounting): closed system $S_2-S_1=\sum Q_j/T_j+S_{gen}$; control volume adds flow terms: $\frac{dS_{cv}}{dt}=\sum\dot m s_{in}-\sum\dot m s_{out}+\sum\dot Q_j/T_j+\dot S_{gen}$.
  • $S_{gen}\ge0$, zero only when everything is reversible. Friction, finite-ΔT heat transfer, mixing — each generates.
  • Principle of increase of entropy (MO2022 END Q.4(b)): system + surroundings together form an isolated universe, so $\Delta S_{universe}=\Delta S_{sys}+\Delta S_{surr}\ge0$ always. This is the final referee for any proposed process: if total entropy would fall, the process is impossible.

11. Availability & Exergy

⏱ 75 min 🏆 5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 10

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: energy is conserved, but useful work is not. Only the part of heat that could drive a reversible engine down to environment temperature is "available"; the rest is taxed away.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Available energy: The maximum portion of a heat supply that can be converted into work by a reversible engine rejecting to the environment: A = Q(1 − T₀/T).
  • Unavailable energy (anergy): The portion of heat that must be rejected to the environment: UA = T₀·ΔS.
  • Dead state: The state in which a system stands in complete thermal, mechanical and chemical equilibrium with its surroundings at p₀, T₀, with no kinetic or potential energy effects.
  • Exergy (availability): The maximum useful work obtainable as a system proceeds reversibly from its given state to the dead state.
  • Irreversibility (lost work): The difference between reversible work and actual work: I = W_rev − W_actual = T₀S_gen (Gouy–Stodola theorem).
  • Second-law efficiency: The ratio of a device’s actual performance to its reversible (ideal) performance for the same task.
  • Available vs unavailable energy of heat: heat Q drawn from a source at T can yield at most Carnot-work against an environment at $T_0$:
$$A=Q\Big(1-\frac{T_0}{T}\Big)\quad(\text{available}),\qquad UA=Q\,\frac{T_0}{T}\quad(\text{unavailable})$$ Cooler source → smaller usable slice of the same heat.
  • Dead state: complete balance with the surroundings — same $p_0$, $T_0$, no velocity, no elevation. Nothing more can be squeezed out once a system reaches it; only departure FROM this state has value.
  • Exergy of a system / stream:
Non-flow exergy: $\phi=(u-u_0)+p_0(v-v_0)-T_0(s-s_0)+\dfrac{V^2}{2}+gz$ Flow exergy: $\psi=(h-h_0)-T_0(s-s_0)+\dfrac{V^2}{2}+gz$ Exergy change between two states = difference of these.
  • Reversible (maximum) work vs useful work: $W_{rev}$ assumes every step reversible down to dead state; $W_{useful}$ additionally discounts the $p_0\Delta V$ push-back against the atmosphere.
  • Gouy–Stodola — lost work: $$I=W_{rev}-W_{actual}=T_0\,S_{gen}$$ Every unit of generated entropy destroys $T_0$ times itself worth of work potential. This single line converts Module 4's entropy accounting into money terms.
  • Second-law efficiency: how close a device runs to its reversible twin: $\eta_{II}=\eta_{act}/\eta_{rev}$ for engines, $COP_{act}/COP_{rev}$ for refrigerators and pumps.
  • Exergy balances mirror energy balances but carry a destruction term I ≥ 0 on the right.

📘 Worked Example

END MO2025 Q.4(b): 0.2 kg air at 300 °C heated reversibly at constant pressure up to 2066 K. With $T_0=303$ K and $c_p=1.0047$ kJ/kg·K, find available and unavailable energy of the heat added.

12. Property Relations — Maxwell, Clapeyron, cp−cv, Joule–Thomson

⏱ 75 min 🏆 5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topics 10–11

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: write four short energy equations ("potentials"), apply one calculus fact — mixed partials of a smooth function are equal — and every property shortcut in this module tumbles out.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Maxwell relations: Four relations connecting partial derivatives of thermodynamic properties, obtained by applying the exact-differential condition (equality of mixed partials) to the potentials u, h, a and g.
  • Clausius–Clapeyron equation: dp/dT |_sat = h_fg/(T·v_fg); it gives the slope of the liquid–vapour saturation line entirely from measurable quantities.
  • Joule–Thomson coefficient: μ_JT = (∂T/∂p)_h — the temperature change per unit pressure drop at constant enthalpy; positive below the inversion temperature (cooling), negative above it (heating).
  • Inversion temperature: The temperature at which the Joule–Thomson coefficient becomes zero, separating the cooling and heating regions.

Step 1 — the four potentials

First + second laws for a simple compressible substance: $$du=Tds-p\,dv \qquad\text{(internal energy)}$$ $$dh=Tds+v\,dp \qquad\text{(enthalpy, } h=u+pv)$$ $$da=-s\,dT-p\,dv \qquad\text{(Helmholtz, } a=u-Ts)$$ $$dg=v\,dp-s\,dT \qquad\text{(Gibbs, } g=h-Ts)$$ Each right side has the form $M\,dx+N\,dy$ — an exact differential.
  • The one calculus fact: if $dz=Mdx+Ndy$ is exact, then $\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right)_x=\left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\right)_y$. Order of differentiation doesn't matter for smooth functions — climb then walk equals walk then climb.

Step 2 — the four Maxwell relations

from du: $\left(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial v}\right)_s=-\left(\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial s}\right)_v$ from dh: $\left(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_s=\;\;\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial s}\right)_p$ from da: $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial v}\right)_T=\;\;\left(\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_v$ from dg: $\left(\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial p}\right)_T=-\left(\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\right)_p$ Pattern check: s always pairs against v or p; T pairs against the other one; signs follow from the −p dv term.

Step 3 — first payoffs: Tds equations & Clapeyron

Insert Maxwell #3 into $ds=\tfrac{1}{T}(du+p\,dv)$: $$Tds=c_vdT+T\Big(\tfrac{\partial p}{\partial T}\Big)_v dv \qquad Tds=c_pdT-T\Big(\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial T}\Big)_p dp$$ Clausius–Clapeyron: on the boiling line $p=p_{sat}(T)$ both phases share g. Differentiate $g_f=g_g$ along the line → $$\boxed{\dfrac{dp}{dT}\bigg|_{sat}=\dfrac{s_{fg}}{v_{fg}}=\dfrac{h_{fg}}{T\,v_{fg}}}$$ Meaning: the slope of the liquid–vapour boundary comes entirely from measurable quantities. Far from critical, $v_{fg}\approx v_g=RT/p$ gives the vapour-pressure form $dp/dT=h_{fg}p/(RT^2)$.

Step 4 — difference & ratio of heat capacities

General result (using Maxwell #3 twice): $$c_p-c_v=-T\,\frac{\big(\partial p/\partial T\big)_v^2}{\big(\partial p/\partial v\big)_T}$$ Ideal-gas check: $(\partial p/\partial T)_v=R/v$ and $(\partial p/\partial v)_T=-p/v$ → $c_p-c_v=-T\cdot\frac{R^2/v^2}{-p/v}=\frac{TR}{v}=R$ ✓ Mayer again. In material constants ($\alpha$ expansion coefficient, $\kappa_T$ isothermal compressibility): $$c_p-c_v=\frac{\alpha^2 v T}{\kappa_T}$$ Ratio: $\gamma=c_p/c_v=\kappa_T/\kappa_s$, the same γ used in adiabatic relations; air γ = 1.4.

Step 5 — Joule–Thomson coefficient

Throttling keeps h constant: set $dh=0$ in $dh=c_pdT+\big[v-T(\partial v/\partial T)_p\big]dp$: $$\mu_{JT}=\Big(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\Big)_h=\frac{T\big(\partial v/\partial T\big)_p-v}{c_p}$$ $\mu_{JT}>0$: gas cools when throttled (most gases at room temperature) $\mu_{JT}<0$: gas warms (hydrogen, helium at room T) $\mu_{JT}=0$: the inversion curve separates the two zones. Ideal gas: $(\partial v/\partial T)_p=v/T$ → numerator zero → $\mu_{JT}=0$. No free cooling from an ideal gas.

📘 Worked Examples (essay answers)

END MO2025 Q.5(a): How does Clausius–Clapeyron give the slope of the liquid–vapour boundary?

Along saturation, phases coexist: $g_f=g_g$. Differentiate with respect to T along the line: $v_f\,dp-s_f\,dT=v_g\,dp-s_g\,dT$ $\Rightarrow\dfrac{dp}{dT}=\dfrac{s_{fg}}{v_{fg}}=\dfrac{h_{fg}}{Tv_{fg}}$ So the slope needs only latent heat and volume change; as critical point nears, $v_{fg}\to0$ and the slope steepens — matching how the dome closes.

END MO2025 Q.5(b): Describe μ_JT and how it tells cooling from heating.

Definition: $\mu_{JT}=(\partial T/\partial p)_h$ — temperature response to a pressure drop at fixed enthalpy. The sign lives in the numerator $N=T(\partial v/\partial T)_p-v$: N > 0 below inversion temperature → throttling cools. N < 0 above it → throttling warms. Ideal gas: N = 0 always — neither effect exists.

13. Supplement — Air-Standard Cycles: Otto & Diesel

⏱ 60 min 🏆 5 marks 🔗 Prereq: Topic 4

📝 Theory Notes

Why this page exists: the printed syllabus puts property relations in Module 5, yet END-sem Q5 asked the Otto/Diesel derivations in 2022 AND 2024. Treat this as the guaranteed-marks appendix — learn both derivations regardless.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Compression ratio, r: r = V₁/V₂ — ratio of total cylinder volume to clearance volume.
  • Clearance volume: The cylinder volume remaining when the piston is at head-end dead centre.
  • Cut-off ratio, ρ: In the Diesel cycle, ρ = V₃/V₂ — the fraction of the stroke during which fuel continues to burn at constant pressure.
  • Air-standard efficiency: The thermal efficiency of the idealised cycle using fixed air as the working fluid: η = 1 − Q_rejected/Q_supplied.
  • Mean effective pressure (MEP): The constant pressure that, acting over one stroke, would produce the same net work as the actual cycle: MEP = W_net/V_swept.
  • Air-standard rules: working fluid stays air (ideal gas, constant c's); combustion becomes heat-from-outside; exhaust becomes heat rejection; every process internally reversible.
  • Otto cycle (petrol engine): 1–2 isentropic compression · 2–3 heat addition at constant volume · 3–4 isentropic expansion · 4–1 heat rejection at constant volume. Compression ratio r = V₁/V₂.
$q_{in}=c_v(T_3-T_2)$, $q_{out}=c_v(T_4-T_1)$; isentropes give $T_4/T_1=T_3/T_2=r^{\gamma-1}$ $$\eta_{Otto}=1-\frac{q_{out}}{q_{in}}=1-\frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}$$ Mean effective pressure: $mep=\dfrac{w_{net}}{v_1-v_2}$ — net work per unit swept volume.
  • Diesel cycle (diesel engine): same shape except heat is added at constant PRESSURE while the piston moves — captured by cut-off ratio ρ = V₃/V₂ ≥ 1.
$$\eta_{Diesel}=1-\frac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}\cdot\frac{\rho^{\gamma}-1}{\gamma(\rho-1)}$$ Same r: Otto beats Diesel (that fraction is > 1). Same peak temperature & pressure: Diesel wins — it may run much larger r without knocking.
