1. Crystals, Defects & Metallography

⏱ 90 min🔗 Prereq: None

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: properties of metals come from how atoms stack. Learn the stacking rules (crystal structures), the mistakes in stacking (defects), and how we photograph the result (metallography) — everything later builds on these.
  • Engineering solids come in three families: metals (metallic bonding, free electrons → conductive, ductile), ceramics (ionic/covalent → hard, brittle, heat-resistant) and polymers (long covalent chains → light, flexible). Composites combine them.
  • Crystalline vs amorphous: crystalline solids repeat their atom pattern over long distances (metals, most ceramics); non-crystalline (glass) has order only between neighbours.
  • Lattice vocabulary: the space lattice is the 3-D grid of points; the unit cell is the smallest repeating block; 14 Bravais lattices fall into 7 crystal systems. Metals mostly use three cells:
BCC (α-Fe, Cr, W): atoms/cell = 2, CN = 8, APF = 0.68, $a = \dfrac{4r}{\sqrt{3}}$ FCC (Al, Cu, Ni, γ-Fe): atoms/cell = 4, CN = 12, APF = 0.74, $a = \dfrac{4r}{\sqrt{2}}$ HCP (Mg, Zn, Ti): atoms/cell = 6, CN = 12, APF = 0.74
  • Miller indices — the recipe: for planes, take intercepts → take reciprocals → clear fractions → bracket $(hkl)$. For directions, take coordinates of head minus tail → clear fractions → bracket $[uvw]$. Negative values get a bar. Families use round brackets () for planes, square [] for directions, angle ⟨⟩ and braces {} for families.
  • Defects, by dimension: point (vacancy, Schottky, Frenkel, interstitial, substitutional solute); line (edge and screw dislocation — the carriers of plastic deformation); surface (grain boundaries, twin boundaries, stacking faults); volume (pores, cracks, inclusions).
  • Why defects matter: strength, conductivity and diffusion are controlled far more by defect populations than by ideal-crystal behaviour. Dislocation motion explains why real metals yield at a fraction of theoretical strength; solid-solution and grain-boundary strengthening work by blocking dislocations.
  • Solid solutions: substitutional (solute replaces solvent atom, similar size — Cu in Ni) or interstitial (small solute fits gaps — C in α-Fe). Both distort the lattice and strengthen the metal.
  • Solidification: melt cools → nucleation (homogeneous in bulk, heterogeneous on impurities/walls — easier because it needs less undercooling) → growth into dendrites → grains meet at boundaries. Pure metals freeze at one temperature; alloys freeze over a range.
  • Metallography workflow: select sample → mount → grind (emery sequence) → polish (alumina) → etch (nital for steel) → examine under optical microscope. Etching attacks boundaries and differentially attacks phases, revealing grain size and microstructure.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Space lattice: An infinite three-dimensional array of points in which every point has identical surroundings.
  • Unit cell: The smallest geometric block which, repeated in space, generates the entire crystal lattice.
  • Atomic packing factor (APF): Ratio of the volume occupied by atoms in a unit cell to the cell volume: APF = (atoms/cell × 4πr³/3)/a³.
  • Coordination number: The number of nearest-neighbour atoms touching any given atom.
  • Dislocation: A line defect along which atoms are misaligned; edge dislocation is an extra half-plane of atoms, screw dislocation a helical distortion.
  • Solid solution: A crystalline phase in which solute atoms enter the solvent lattice either substitutionally or interstitially without changing the basic structure.
  • Heterogeneous nucleation: Nucleation that occurs preferentially on pre-existing surfaces (mould walls, impurities) because the energy barrier is lower than for homogeneous nucleation.

📘 Worked Example

(a) APF of simple cubic: one corner atom contributes ⅛ × 8 = 1 atom; $a = 2r$ APF = $\dfrac{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}}{(2r)^3} = \dfrac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.52$ (b) Direction [110] in FCC: from origin, move 1a along x, 1a along y, 0 along z — it lies in the dense diagonal; planar density along (111) is highest, which is why FCC metals are ductile and slip on {111}<110>.

2 · Gibbs Rule, Lever Rule & Iron–Carbon Diagram

2. Gibbs Rule, Lever Rule & Iron–Carbon Diagram

⏱ 90 min🔗 Prereq: Topic 1

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: a phase diagram is a map telling you which phases exist at any temperature-combination point, and in what amounts. The Fe–C diagram is the single most valuable map in metallurgy — it explains steel AND cast iron.
  • Gibbs phase rule: $P + F = C + 2$ (general); for constant pressure metallurgy use the condensed rule $P + F = C + 1$. At a eutectic point (P = 3, C = 2) → F = 0: temperature is fixed and all three phases coexist — an invariant reaction.
  • Binary diagram families: isomorphous (complete mutual solubility — Cu-Ni); eutectic (liquid freezes to two solids at once — Pb-Sn); peritectic (liquid + solid → different solid); monotectic (liquid → liquid + solid). Eutectoid is the same idea fully in solid state — the heart of the Fe-C story.
  • Lever rule gives phase fractions at any two-phase point: measure tie-line endpoints $C_\alpha$, $C_\beta$ and overall composition $C_0$:
$W_\alpha = \dfrac{C_\beta - C_0}{C_\beta - C_\alpha}, \qquad W_\beta = \dfrac{C_0 - C_\alpha}{C_\beta - C_\alpha}$
  • Fe–Fe₃C landmarks (memorise): eutectoid 727 °C at 0.76 %C (γ → α + Fe₃C = pearlite); eutectic 1147 °C at 4.3 %C (liquid → austenite + cementite = ledeburite); max carbon in γ-austenite 2.11 % at 1147 °C. Steel ≤ 2.11 %C, cast iron above.
  • Phases: ferrite α (BCC, soft, magnetic, dissolves almost no C), austenite γ (FCC, dissolves up to 2.11 %C, non-magnetic), cementite Fe₃C (6.67 %C, very hard brittle intermetallic), pearlite (layered α + Fe₃C eutectoid mix), martensite (not on this diagram! — supersaturated BCT made by fast quench).
  • Hypo- vs hyper-eutectoid: below 0.76 %C primary ferrite forms first, leaving pearlite; above it, cementite networks form first. Microstructure reading follows directly.
  • Alloying elements either dissolve in ferrite (Ni strengthens), form carbides (Cr, Mo, V), stabilise austenite (Ni, Mn — enabling stainless austenitics) or stabilise ferrite (Cr, Si). Steels gain hardenability mainly from Mn, Cr, Mo, Ni.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Phase: A physically distinct, chemically homogeneous region of matter having its own structure and properties, separated from other regions by a boundary.
  • Component: The minimum number of chemically independent species needed to describe the composition of every phase in the system.
  • Gibbs phase rule: P + F = C + 2 (or P + F = C + 1 at constant pressure); F is the number of variables (temperature, composition) that can be changed independently without changing the number of phases.
  • Eutectic reaction: A liquid transforms on cooling into two simultaneous solid phases: L → α + β at fixed temperature and composition.
  • Eutectoid reaction: One solid phase transforms into two other solid phases on cooling: γ → α + Fe₃C at 727 °C, producing pearlite.
  • Pearlite: The lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite formed by the eutectoid decomposition of austenite.
  • Lever rule: A mass-balance method giving the weight fraction of each phase at a point inside a two-phase field, proportional to tie-line lengths opposite that phase.

📘 Worked Example

Phases just below 727 °C in a 0.4 %C steel: tie-line runs from $C_\alpha$ = 0.022 %C (ferrite) to $C_{Fe_3C}$ = 6.67 %C (cementite). Ferrite fraction = $\dfrac{6.67 - 0.4}{6.67 - 0.022} = \dfrac{6.27}{6.648} \approx 94.3\ \%$ Cementite ≈ 5.7 %. (Pearlite fraction uses the 0.76 endpoint instead: $\dfrac{0.4-0.022}{0.76-0.022}\approx51\ \%$ pearlite.)

3 · TTT/CCT & Heat Treatment of Steel

3. TTT/CCT Curves & Heat Treatment of Steel

⏱ 90 min🔗 Prereq: Topic 2

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: the Fe–C diagram says WHAT phases are stable; TTT/CCT curves say HOW FAST you must cool to get them. Heat treatment is engineering the cooling path.
  • TTT (isothermal) diagram: austenite held at a fixed temperature decomposes after an incubation time — the C-curve. Nose ≈ 550 °C: above it diffusion makes pearlite/bainite; below $M_s$ (~230 °C for eutectoid steel) diffusionless martensite forms instantly.
  • CCT diagram: real cooling is continuous, so transformation lines shift right and down; critical cooling rate = fastest line that avoids the nose entirely → 100 % martensite.
  • Four basic treatments: annealing (slow furnace cool → coarse pearlite, softest, stress-free); normalizing (air cool → finer pearlite, grain refinement); hardening (quench past the nose → martensite, hardest but brittle and stressed); tempering (reheat martensite at 200–650 °C → fine carbides precipitate, toughness returns at some strength cost).
  • Hardenability ≠ hardness: hardenability is the DEPTH to which martensite forms; measured by the Jominy end-quench test (water-jet one end of a bar, plot HRC vs distance). Carbon and alloying elements (Mn, Cr, Mo) push the C-curve right → deeper hardening.
  • Cold vs hot working: deforming above the recrystallization temperature = hot working (no strain hardening, refined grains); below it = cold working (strain-hardened, stronger but less ductile). On heating a cold-worked metal: recovery first (residual stress relief, same grains), then recrystallization (new strain-free grains), finally grain growth.
  • Surface hardening: carburizing (diffuse C into the skin near 900 °C, then quench), nitriding (N near 500 °C, no quench needed), flame/induction hardening (fast surface reheat + quench). Result: hard wear-resistant case over a tough core.
  • Quenching stages: vapour-blanket (slow start) → nucleate boiling (fastest heat removal) → convection (slow finish). Severity order: still air < oil < water < brine. Heat-treatment defects: distortion, quench cracks (too severe a quench), soft spots (vapour pockets), oxidation/decarburization, overheating/burning.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • TTT diagram: A time–temperature–transformation curve showing when austenite begins and finishes decomposing while held isothermally at each temperature.
  • Critical cooling rate: The minimum continuous cooling rate that bypasses the nose of the CCT curve so austenite transforms entirely to martensite.
  • Martensite: A supersaturated diffusionless body-centred-tetragonal phase formed when austenite is quenched fast enough; the hardest constituent of steel.
  • Tempering: Reheating quenched martensitic steel below the eutectoid temperature to relieve stresses and precipitate fine carbides, trading hardness for toughness.
  • Hardenability: The capability of a steel to harden to depth by martensitic transformation on quenching, measured by the Jominy end-quench test.
  • Recrystallization temperature: The approximate temperature (about 0.3–0.5 Tm in kelvin) at which new strain-free equiaxed grains replace cold-worked grains within about one hour.
  • Case hardening: Any process (carburizing, nitriding, flame/induction hardening) producing a hard wear-resistant surface layer over a tough core.

📘 Worked Example

Estimate the recrystallization temperature of copper ($T_m$ = 1085 °C = 1358 K): $T_{rec} \approx 0.4\,T_m = 543\ \text{K} = \mathbf{270^{\circ}\text{C}}$ So rolling Cu at room temperature is genuinely "cold working", while Cu hot-working needs only about 300 °C upward.

4. Cast Irons, Steels, Non-Ferrous Alloys & Ceramics

⏱ 60 min🔗 Prereq: Topic 2

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: this module is a catalogue with logic — every alloy family exists because someone engineered a property trade-off on purpose. Learn each family as composition → microstructure → property → typical use.
  • Cast irons (2.1–4.3 %C): grey (flake graphite — excellent damping and machinability, weak in tension; machine beds); spheroidal-graphite/ductile (magnesium-treated nodules — near-steel ductility; crankshafts); white (carbon as cementite — very hard, brittle; rolls and liners); malleable (white iron annealed long so graphite forms as clumps). Cooling rate decides grey vs white: slow → grey, fast → white.
  • Stainless steels (>10.5 %Cr): austenitic 18Cr-8Ni type 304 (non-magnetic, weldable, top corrosion resistance — kitchens, chemical plants); ferritic 409/430 (cheaper, magnetic — exhaust systems); martensitic 410/440 (quench-hardenable — knives, turbine blades); duplex combines austenitic + ferritic strengths.
  • Maraging steels: extra-low-carbon Fe-Ni alloys hardened by precipitating intermetallics during aging rather than by carbon — very high strength with good toughness (rocket casings, die tooling).
  • Superalloys: nickel-based, strengthened by ordered γ′ precipitates Ni₃(Al,Ti); hold useful strength close to 1000 °C — jet-engine hot sections.
  • Non-ferrous families: aluminium alloys (light + corrosion-proof — 2xxx/7xxx aerospace, cast 3xx engine blocks); copper alloys (brass = Cu-Zn for free-machining, bronze = Cu-Sn for bearings); titanium alloys (best strength-to-weight, biocompatible); magnesium/zinc die-casting alloys; nickel superalloys.
  • Engineering ceramics: oxides (Al₂O₃ refractories), carbides/nitrides (WC cutting tools, Si₃N₄ bearings, SiC heating elements), silicates (glass, porcelain). Usually powder-pressed then sintered. Strong in compression, brittle in tension, excellent at high temperature.
  • Engineering polymers: thermoplastics soften repeatedly on heating (PE, PP, nylon); thermosets cross-link once and then char instead of melting (epoxy, phenolic). Light, low-friction and corrosion-immune, but low stiffness and limited service temperature.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Grey cast iron: Cast iron in which carbon solidifies as flake graphite owing to slow cooling and silicon content; high compressive strength, self-lubricating and strongly vibration-damping.
  • Spheroidal-graphite (ductile) iron: Cast iron treated with magnesium so graphite grows as spheroids, giving near-steel ductility and toughness.
  • Austenitic stainless steel: A Cr-Ni steel (e.g. 18 % Cr, 8 % Ni) retaining face-centred-cubic austenite at room temperature — non-magnetic, not hardenable by quenching, outstandingly corrosion-resistant.
  • Maraging steel: An extra-low-carbon, high-nickel alloy steel strengthened mainly by precipitation of intermetallic compounds during aging.
  • Superalloy: A heat-resistant alloy, usually nickel-based strengthened by ordered γ′ precipitates, able to carry load above about 600 °C.
  • Sintering: Bonding of compacted powder particles by diffusion at high temperature below the melting point — the forming route for ceramics and powder-metallurgy parts.

5. Mechanical Testing, Fatigue, Creep & Corrosion

⏱ 75 min🔗 Prereq: None

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: design needs numbers and numbers come from tests: pull it (tensile), dent it (hardness), snap it (impact), shake-load it millions of times (fatigue), hold it hot for years (creep), and watch it rust (corrosion).
  • Tensile test gives: elastic modulus $E=\sigma/\epsilon$ from the slope, yield strength (0.2 % offset rule), ultimate tensile strength (peak load ÷ original area), % elongation (ductility), toughness (area under the curve). Necking begins just after UTS.
  • Hardness scales: Brinell — steel ball indent, good for coarse/soft materials; Rockwell — measures indent DEPTH, dial readout, fastest for steel (HRC); Vickers — square diamond pyramid, one continuous scale for everything soft to hard.
$HB = \dfrac{2F}{\pi D\left(D-\sqrt{D^{2}-d^{2}}\right)}$ $\sigma = \dfrac{P}{A_0}$ engineering stress · $\epsilon = \dfrac{\Delta L}{L_0}$ Wear: adhesive · abrasive · erosive · corrosive · fretting · fatigue
  • Impact tests (Charpy/Izod): energy absorbed by breaking a notched bar tracks the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature. BCC steels turn brittle when cold; FCC metals stay tough.
  • Fatigue: failure under repeated stress far below yield. The S–N curve plots stress amplitude vs cycles-to-failure; steels show an endurance limit near half the UTS below which life is effectively infinite. Cracks start at notches, scratches and fillets — hence surface finish matters. Factors: stress concentration, mean stress, surface condition, environment.
  • Creep: slow permanent strain under steady load at high temperature. Three stages: primary (slowing down), secondary (steady state — the design region), tertiary (accelerating to rupture).
  • Corrosion is electrochemical: the anode dissolves while the cathode survives; the galvanic series predicts which metal suffers in a couple. Forms: uniform, galvanic, pitting, crevice, intergranular (sensitised stainless near welds), stress-corrosion cracking. Prevention: coatings, inhibitors, cathodic protection with sacrificial Zn/Mg anodes, sensible design (no crevices), right material choice.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Yield strength: The stress at which a material begins to deform plastically, taken from a 0.2 % offset construction on the stress–strain curve when no sharp knee exists.
  • Endurance limit: The maximum stress amplitude below which a ferrous material endures an infinite number of load cycles without fatigue failure.
  • Creep: Time-dependent permanent deformation under sustained load, significant above roughly 0.4 Tm.
  • Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature: The temperature below which a normally ductile BCC metal fractures in brittle fashion under impact.
  • Galvanic corrosion: Accelerated attack of the less-noble of two dissimilar metals electrically connected in an electrolyte.
  • Cathodic protection: Corrosion prevention by forcing the structure to be the cathode, using either sacrificial anodes or impressed current.

📘 Worked Example

A 50 kN tensile load acts on a rod of diameter 12.7 mm: $A_0=\dfrac{\pi}{4}(12.7)^2=126.7\ \text{mm}^2$ $\sigma=\dfrac{50000}{126.7}=\mathbf{394.6\ MPa}$ A 50 mm gauge length stretches 0.06 mm elastically → $E=\dfrac{\sigma}{\epsilon}=\dfrac{394.6}{0.06/50}\approx\mathbf{329\ GPa}$
