1 · Sand, Die, Centrifugal & Investment Casting

1. Sand, Die, Centrifugal & Investment Casting

⏱ 75 min🔗 Prereq: None

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: casting = pour liquid metal into a shaped cavity and let it freeze. Every variant differs only in HOW the cavity is made and HOW the metal gets there.
  • Sand casting route: make a pattern (a slightly oversized copy of the part) → pack sand around it in flasks → remove pattern → add core for internal cavities → close mould → pour → shake out → fettle. Cheap tooling, any metal, but rough surface and loose tolerance.
  • Pattern allowances (why patterns are bigger/odd-shaped): shrinkage (metal contracts on freezing — add ~1–2 % by metal), machining (extra stock where surfaces will be cut), draft (taper so the pattern lifts out cleanly), distortion (compensate warpage of thin odd shapes), rattle/shake (for easy-collapsing sand shapes).
  • Gating system jobs: pouring basin (receive and settle the stream) → sprue (vertical channel; tapered to avoid air aspiration) → runner (distribute) → gate (control entry speed/direction) → riser (molten reserve that feeds shrinkage during solidification) plus core and vents. Chvorinov's rule says flat/thick sections freeze slowest:
$t_s = B\left(\dfrac{V}{A}\right)^{n}$ , n ≈ 2 — risers are made fat ($V/A$ large) so they stay liquid longest. Shrinkage allowance = dimension × shrinkage % (e.g. 100 mm Al casting @ 1.3 % → pattern 101.3 mm)
  • Centrifugal casting: spin the mould; true centrifugal makes pipe-like parts with no core (centrifugal force flings metal outward, impurities inward to be machined off); semi-centrifugal uses cores for shaped parts; centrifuged arranges many small moulds around one spinning table.
  • Die casting: permanent steel dies + high pressure. Hot chamber (plunger sits IN the melt — gooseneck): fast cycle, only low-melting alloys (Zn, Mg, Pb). Cold chamber (ladle pours each shot into a cold shot sleeve): handles higher-melting Al, Cu alloys because the injection unit stays out of the melt.
  • Investment casting (lost-wax): inject wax patterns → assemble on a tree → dip in ceramic slurry, build shell → melt out wax → fire shell → pour. Near-net-shape, superb finish, complex geometry (turbine blades), but expensive per part.

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Pattern: A replica of the object to be cast, slightly enlarged by allowances, used to form the mould cavity in sand.
  • Riser (feed head): A reservoir of molten metal in the mould that feeds the casting during solidification to compensate shrinkage.
  • Draft allowance: The taper given to vertical pattern faces so the pattern can be withdrawn without tearing the mould walls.
  • Hot-chamber die casting: A die-casting process in which the injection plunger operates immersed in the molten-metal pot, suited to low-melting alloys like zinc.
  • Investment casting: A precision casting process using expendable wax patterns surrounded by a ceramic shell which is emptied of wax before pouring.
  • Chvorinov's rule: Solidification time is proportional to the square of volume-to-surface-area ratio: t = B(V/A)².

📘 Worked Example

A cube side 100 mm and a sphere of equal volume are cast from the same alloy: cube $V/A = a/6 = 16.7$ mm → $t \propto (16.7)^2 = 278$ sphere $V/A = D/6$ with $D=(6V/\pi)^{1/3}=124$ mm → $V/A=20.7$ mm → $t\propto427$ The sphere freezes ≈1.54× slower — shape alone changes feeding behaviour.

2 · Tool Geometry, Chips, Forces & Tool Life

2. Tool Geometry, Chips, Forces & Tool Life

⏱ 75 min🔗 Prereq: None

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: cutting metal = shearing a thin layer ahead of a wedge. Everything — angles, chip type, forces, wear, life — follows from that shear zone.
  • Single-point tool angles (ASA system): back rake (top face slope — positive eases flow), side rake, end/side relief (clearance so only the edge touches), end/side cutting-edge angles, nose radius. Higher rake → lower force, weaker tip.
  • Orthogonal cutting model: cutting edge ⊥ cutting velocity (2-D analysis); oblique = inclined edge (real life, 3-D forces).
  • Chip types: discontinuous (brittle materials / low speed — segments), continuous (ductile + high speed + good lubrication — ideal finish), continuous with built-up edge (low-speed ductile cutting; BUE welds onto rake face, ruins finish, periodically breaks off), serrated (thermal chips in Ti superalloys).
  • Shear-plane relations: chip thickness ratio $r=t_1/t_2$ (uncut/chip thickness, always < 1); shear angle from Merchant:
$\tan\phi=\dfrac{r\cos\alpha}{1-r\sin\alpha}$ Merchant: $\phi = 45^{\circ}+\dfrac{\alpha}{2}-\dfrac{\beta}{2}$ (α rake, β friction angle) Cutting power: $P = F_c V$ ; specific energy $u = \dfrac{F_c V}{MRR}$
  • Taylor tool-life equation — the exam favourite:
$VT^{n}=C \Rightarrow T=\Big(\dfrac{C}{V}\Big)^{1/n}$ — V cutting speed, T tool life (min). Higher n = tougher material response; C = cutting speed giving 1-min life.
  • Tool failure modes: gradual flank wear (measure land width VB ≈ 0.3 mm criterion), crater wear on rake (diffusion at high speed), sudden fracture (excessive force/shock), plastic deformation of edge, thermal cracking (intermittent cuts).
  • Tool materials ladder: HSS (cheap, tough, ≤ 30 m/min steel) → uncoated/carbide inserts (WC-Co, 3–5× HSS speeds) → coated carbide (TiN/TiAlN — standard today) → ceramics/CMC (very hot, brittle) → CBN (hardened steels) → diamond (nonferrous, graphite, composites — never ferrous: carbon affinity).

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Orthogonal cutting: A simplified two-dimensional cutting arrangement in which the cutting edge is perpendicular to the direction of cutting velocity.
  • Built-up edge (BUE): A layer of work material pressure-welded onto the rake face during cutting of ductile metals at low speed, which alters the effective geometry and degrades surface finish.
  • Chip thickness ratio: Ratio of uncut chip thickness to chip thickness, r = t₁/t₂, always less than 1 because the chip shortens as it thickens.
  • Taylor's tool-life equation: VTⁿ = C relating cutting speed V and tool life T; n depends on tool–work pair and C on cutting conditions.
  • Flank wear: Wear land developed on the relief face of the tool behind the cutting edge, used as the standard tool-failure criterion (VB ≈ 0.3 mm).
  • Coolant/cutting fluid roles: Cooling, lubrication, chip flushing and corrosion protection of the freshly cut surface.

📘 Worked Example

Carbide tool: V₁ = 60 m/min gives T₁ = 30 min; n = 0.25. $C = V_1T_1^{\,n} = 60\times30^{0.25} = 60\times2.340 = 140.4$ Life at V₂ = 80 m/min: $T_2 = \left(\dfrac{140.4}{80}\right)^{1/0.25} = (1.755)^{4} \approx \mathbf{9.5\ min}$ Speed up 33 %, life drops 68 % — Taylor in action.

3. Lathe, Shaper, Milling, Drilling & Grinding

⏱ 75 min🔗 Prereq: Topic 2

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: a machine tool is defined by which motion spins (cutting) and which slides (feed). Lathe: work rotates. Shaper: tool reciprocates. Milling/drilling: tool rotates. Grinding: abrasive wheel rotates.
  • Lathe: headstock (spindle + speed box), bed (guideways), carriage/saddle (feed), tailstock (supports long jobs / drills), leadscrew for threads. Operations: turning, facing, taper turning (compound-rest swivel or tailstock offset), knurling, thread cutting. Key formulas:
Cutting speed: $V=\dfrac{\pi D N}{1000}$ m/min (D mm, N rpm) Turning time: $T=\dfrac{L}{f\,N}$ (f feed mm/rev) Tailstock offset for taper: $S=\dfrac{D-d}{2}\times\dfrac{L_{work}}{l}$
  • Shaper: ram carries the tool on quick-return mechanism (Whitworth/crank-slotted lever — cutting stroke slow, return fast ≈ 2:1); table feeds at each stroke; used for flat surfaces, slots, keyways. Stroke length = job length + 20–25 mm approach/over-travel.
  • Milling: multi-tooth rotating cutter. Up-milling (conventional — cutter against feed, chip thickens from zero, better for hard skins) vs down/climb milling (with feed, thick-to-thin chip, better finish but needs backlash-free machine). Operations: slabbing, face milling, slotting, gear cutting with dividing head.
  • Drilling: twist drill has two cutting lips, chisel edge (no real cutting — pushes), margins for guiding; operations: drilling, reaming (sizing), boring (enlarging/truing), tapping (internal threads).
  • Grinding: abrasive wheel spec = abrasive | grit | grade | structure | bond (e.g. A-46-K-5-V). Soft materials → hard grades hold grits longer; hard materials → soft grades release dull grits (self-sharpening). Processes: surface, cylindrical, internal, centreless (through-feed small rollers/pins).

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Taper turning: Producing a conical surface on a lathe by compound-rest swivelling or tailstock offset so that the tool travels at the half-cone angle.
  • Quick-return mechanism: The shaper drive that makes the cutting stroke slower than the idle return stroke, reducing non-cutting time.
  • Climb (down) milling: Cutter rotation direction same as feed direction; chip starts thick and ends thin, giving better finish but requiring rigid backlash-free machines.
  • Grain size (grit number): Sieve count defining abrasive particle size in a grinding wheel — larger number = finer grain.
  • Self-sharpening: Property of a correctly chosen soft-grade wheel in which dulled grains fracture or pull out, exposing fresh sharp abrasive.

📘 Worked Example

Turn a 60 mm bar down a 200 mm length at N = 300 rpm, f = 0.3 mm/rev: $V=\dfrac{\pi\times60\times300}{1000}=\mathbf{56.5\ m/min}$ $T=\dfrac{L}{fN}=\dfrac{200}{0.3\times300}=\mathbf{2.22\ min}$ Shaper: job 120 mm → stroke = 150 mm; if ratio 2:1 and cutting 25 strokes/min, average cutting velocity uses forward-stroke share only.

4. Rolling, Forging, Extrusion & Sheet-Metal Forming

⏱ 75 min🔗 Prereq: Materials M3 (recrystallization)

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: forming squeezes metal into shape instead of shaving it away — no chips, refined grain flow, stronger parts. Temperature decides everything: hot working above recrystallization, cold below it.
  • Hot vs cold working: hot — no strain hardening, big shape changes, low forces, but scale/rough finish; cold — strain-hardened strength, tight tolerance, good finish, but higher forces and limited reduction per pass.
  • Rolling: stock squeezed between counter-rotating rolls. Maximum draft (one-pass bite) set by friction:
Max draft: $\Delta h_{max}=\mu^{2}R$ · Contact length $L=\sqrt{R\,\Delta h}$ Roll force $F=\bar{\sigma}\,w\,L$ (mean flow stress × width × contact length) Rolling mills: two-high (reversing), three-high, four-high (+backup rolls), cluster/tandem trains. Products: plates, strips, rails, I-beams, threaded rod.
  • Forging: open-die (simple flat/platen tools — big ingots, operator skill shapes it), closed/impression die (matched cavities + gutter flash that blocks out and ensures fill), press vs hammer (slow squeeze vs impact). Defects: cold shuts, laps, unfilled sections, die mismatch.
  • Extrusion: push billet through a die — direct (ram moves billet, high friction) vs indirect (die moves toward stationary billet, ~30 % lower force); hot extrusion at ~⅔ Tm; impact extrusion cold for tubes/cans. Extrusion ratio:
$R=\dfrac{A_o}{A_e}$ — typical hot-extrusion range 10–100; force rises with R and flow stress.
  • Sheet-metal operations: blanking (the FALLER... careful: the cut-out piece IS the product) vs piercing/punching (the hole matters, slug is scrap) — clearance ~5–10 % of thickness per side; deep drawing draws a cup through a die — watch drawing ratio d/p ≤ ~2 and blank-holder pressure to stop wrinkling; bending leaves springback (over-bend to compensate, bend radius ≥ ~1t).

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Recrystallization (in forming): Formation of new strain-free equiaxed grains in a deformed metal when heated above its recrystallization temperature, replacing the strain-hardened structure.
  • Maximum draft: Greatest possible reduction in one rolling pass, Δh = μ²R, beyond which the rolls merely skid instead of biting the stock.
  • Flash: The thin excess metal squeezed out around the parting line in closed-die forging, acting as a pressure seal that forces metal to fill the die cavity.
  • Extrusion ratio: Ratio of billet cross-section area to extruded product area, R = Ao/Ae, a measure of deformation severity.
  • Blanking vs piercing: In blanking the punched-out piece is the product; in piercing the surrounding sheet is the product and the punched slug is scrap.
  • Springback: Elastic recovery of sheet metal after bending, causing the final angle to open slightly beyond the die angle; compensated by over-bending.

📘 Worked Example

Steel strip rolled on 300 mm-radius rolls with μ = 0.25: $\Delta h_{max}=0.25^{2}\times300=18.75$ mm (theoretical bite limit) For an actual pass Δh = 9 mm: contact length $L=\sqrt{300\times9}=\mathbf{52\ mm}$ Extrude Ø150 mm billet to Ø30 mm rod: $R=\dfrac{150^2}{30^2}=\mathbf{25}$ — well within hot-extrusion practice.

Module 5 · Topics
5 · Gas, Arc, Resistance Welding & Brazing

5. Gas, Arc, Resistance Welding & Soldering/Brazing

⏱ 60 min🔗 Prereq: None

📝 Theory Notes

Big idea: welding melts parent metal (fusion) or squeezes hot plastic metal together (resistance); brazing/soldering join WITHOUT melting parents using a filler with lower melting point. Pick process by metal, thickness and position.
  • Oxy-acetylene flame: oxygen + C₂H₂ burn at ≈3100–3300 °C. Flame types: neutral (equal volumes — general steel work), carburising/reducing (excess acetylene — hardfacing, Al), oxidising (excess O₂ — brass/Zn bronzes, prevents Zn fuming loss).
  • SMAW/MMAW (stick): consumable flux-coated electrode; flux burns → shielding gas + slag cover + stabilisers; simplest, any position, outdoor-friendly; electrode changes interrupt work.
  • SAW (submerged arc): bare wire under granular flux blanket; deep penetration, very high deposition, flat/horizontal only — pressure vessels, beams, ship plate.
  • GTAW (TIG): non-consumable tungsten + argon shield; filler added by hand; supreme quality/control — stainless, Ti, aerospace thin sections, root passes.
  • GMAW (MIG/MAG): continuously-fed wire electrode through gun with Ar/CO₂ shield; fast, all positions, easy to learn; CO₂ cheap mode = spatter + slightly rougher bead.
  • Resistance welding: press parts between copper electrodes, pass huge current for milliseconds — heat Q = I²Rt concentrates at interface contact resistance → nugget forms. Spot (sheets, auto bodies), seam (overlapping spots via roller electrodes), projection (nuts/bosses).
Spot-weld heat: $H=I^{2}RT$ joules e.g. I = 8000 A, R = 100 µΩ, T = 0.2 s → H = 64×10⁶ × 10⁻⁴ × 0.2 = \mathbf{1280\ J}
  • Soldering vs brazing: both bond with filler melting BELOW parent metals' solidus and NO base-metal fusion. Solder: filler melts < 450 °C (Sn-Pb, Sn-Ag — electronics, plumbing). Braze: filler > 450 °C (Cu-Zn brass, Ag alloys — stronger joints, torch/furnace heated); flux removes oxides in both; joint strength comes from capillary gap design (~0.05–0.15 mm).

📖 Exam Definitions — quote these in your answers

  • Neutral flame: Oxy-acetylene flame with chemically balanced gases producing no oxidising or carburising effect — standard for steel welding.
  • Flux: A material applied during welding/brazing that cleans the joint area by removing oxides, shields the molten pool, and improves wetting.
  • GTAW (TIG): An arc-welding process using a non-consumable tungsten electrode shielded by inert argon/helium, with optional hand-fed filler — highest control and quality.
  • Resistance spot welding: Joining overlapping sheets by the heat of I²R generated at their contact interface while copper electrodes clamp them, forming a weld nugget.
  • Brazing: A joining process in which a filler metal with liquidus above 450 °C but below the parent metals' solidus is drawn into a close-fitting joint by capillary action without melting the parent metal.
  • Weld decay: Intergranular corrosion of sensitised austenitic stainless steel in the heat-affected zone near welds, prevented by low-carbon grades or Ti/Nb stabilisation.
